A score you can argue with

Match Moth reads a job seeker's resume and scores every listing against it. It has been in open beta since August 2026, in one of the roughest tech markets in years. The design problem underneath is the one this page is about: making an AI judgment legible enough that a stranger acts on it.

The Problem

Search is an attention problem

Job seekers spend about six hours a week on the search, and most of it goes to filtering, not pursuing. In 2026 polling, 46% named "finding opportunities that match my qualifications" as their top challenge, ahead of pay or interviews (Express Employment Professionals / Harris Poll). The right roles exist. Finding yours in the noise is the work, and mainstream tools push that work onto the person with the least time to spare.

The market's answer is volume. Mass-apply tools promise fifty applications a night, and they sell because the pain is real. The results are not. Apply-to-interview rates have collapsed as the flood rose, and every automated application makes the next one worth less. Match Moth is the opposite bet: fewer, better-aimed applications, chosen with a score you can argue with.

That framing is why I wanted to build it. It's LLM-as-judge with a human making the final call, the same evaluation-interface problem I design for professionally. The criticism here is aimed at the tools. A seeker who sent 300 applications did the rational thing with the lever they had.

Origin

It started as a tool for one

The first version had my resume hardcoded, a nightly crawl of a few hundred companies, and a ranked email every morning. It worked. I reviewed a shortlist in minutes instead of scrolling for an hour, and the roles I actually wanted rose to the top on their own. Then I opened the door to other people, and nearly every decision below came from what happened when they walked through it.

AI Job-Fit

Opening the black box

What a number means

A single 0 to 100 is a verdict, and a verdict invites exactly one question: why? So the score is five factors, with the weights shown on every breakdown: skills 35, seniority 20, compensation 20, industry 15, location 10. Two come from the model, skills and industry, because they need a reading of the posting against the resume. Three are deterministic: the distance between the listing's seniority band and the user's target, the salary against the user's floor, and the work mode and metro against the user's preferences. The weights haven't changed since June 2026. What changed since is how they're explained.

Honest absence

Renormalizing has a limit. When enough is missing, the number stops meaning much, and the product has to say so instead of rounding up. Every score carries a confidence tier: full, partial, or low. Partial means less than half the weight was grounded. Low means the skills factor itself couldn't be evaluated, and a low-confidence score is capped at 65 and excluded from the top bands entirely. As of September, 27% of all scores land below full confidence: 22% partial, 5% low.

That's the decision I'd defend hardest. A competitor's "98% match" that I once traced in a network tab was a display number sitting on an internal score of about 0.5. A gap on a screen looks worse than a fake number. I think the gap is the product. A seeker who sees "couldn't evaluate" learns something true about the posting, and that the score won't flatter them.

Verdict and gates

Under every number sits a one-line verdict in plain language, and above it three chips: remote, compensation, and IC versus management, each pass or warn. Chips beat prose on a card because they answer the three questions a seeker asks before reading anything: whether it's remote, whether it pays enough, and whether it's their kind of role. If a chip warns, the reader knows before the score does.

Bands

Scores band into Off-target below 50, Worth a look from 50, Promising from 70, and Standout from 85. The first feed a new user sees targets Promising and above.

Most listings are mediocre fits, and the score says so. The median real user's median score is 55, below the feed floor. The bands appear to mean something to people: real users save or apply to Standout listings at 2.0%, against 0.14% for Promising, about 14 times the rate. It's also only two and a half weeks of open beta, there's no impression log yet, and the denominator is every scored row rather than every row seen.

At Scale

What changed when strangers showed up

Per-user scoring means per-user cost. One person's score is worth nothing to anyone else, so every listing is judged against every resume. A recall-tuned keyword gate runs first, so only directionally fit job listings reach the model, and hard per-user and global spend stops sit underneath it. That works out to about $0.08 in AI cost per user per day.

Data quality is a UX feature. The feed is only as good as what feeds it. Today that is 50+ sources of three kinds across 26,685 companies: ATS boards, employer careers sites, and aggregators and remote boards. 81% of live listings come directly from ATS boards and careers sites, the canonical copy before a role syndicates out and goes stale. 61.6% carry a salary. Dead listings are closed nightly: 30,555 in the last 30 days, 12% of the corpus.

The Workday story. Workday returns HTTP 200 after a job is gone, so the URL validator never closed one. I browser-verified all 17,166 live Workday rows, closed 469, then made the nightly reaper check each Workday listing the way a browser would. "Know someone there" shipped in the same stretch: import a LinkedIn connections file and each listing shows who you already know at that company, parsed in the browser, with no email addresses uploaded.

Onboarding

The onboarding bet I got wrong

How people arrived. I posted my own story on LinkedIn and Reddit, and replied as a person in threads where people were describing the same job-search pain, with no pitch unless someone asked. One Reddit post reached about 5,000 views, sat briefly at the top of its subreddit, and brought 15 signups in 24 hours. Most of them arrived on a phone, from inside a social app, which is the context for everything below.

The bet. Resume-first and mandatory. The resume is the primary scoring evidence, so the product asked for it before showing anything, and pre-filled preferences from it so setup took two minutes. I bet the value was obvious enough to earn the upload up front.

What users did. 38% of real signups uploaded a resume. Until the skip shipped, that meant 62% of signups never saw a feed. Every deferral happened at the resume step, and 10 of 12 were on a phone. Every resume that was uploaded parsed cleanly, whether PDF, DOCX, or a LinkedIn import. The parser was fine. Finding and uploading a file on a phone before seeing a single job was the wall.

The decision. Three changes in one week: browse before setup, then an upload-or-browse fork with both cards listing what you get and what you give up, then "Skip to jobs" on step one. The tradeoff is stated to the user in plain terms. A skipper gets an unscored feed, and the product says so.

The result, honestly. Nineteen sessions since the skip shipped. Resume-step completion moved, but three changes landed in one week and I can't claim causation for any one of them. Next is skip-reason capture and per-step events, then a real before and after.

Operations

Designing for the operator too

A live product has a second user, the person who runs it. An admin status page shows queue depth, AI spend against its caps, captured exceptions, and email delivery health, so a bad morning is diagnosable before a user reports it. A dev-only design route renders real components on the real app canvas, one section per state, so visual decisions are judged against the actual background rather than a mockup.

The daily digest is a bounded contract: up to five new roles, up to three reminders, and any listing shown at most three times ever. That hasn't changed since June. Three beta reports since September 1, on profile fields, the resume editor, and a dead Workday link, each shipped the same day or within days.

The Craft

From a tool to a brand

The first UI was a component-library default and looked like one. For a tool I'd open every morning that wasn't good enough, so it went through three generations in two months: an unstyled proof of concept, a DaisyUI pass that made it usable, and a dark rebuild in the style of my portfolio, with the interaction details that make something feel considered.

The fourth generation came with strangers. A tool for one can borrow its owner's taste, but once strangers were using it, it needed a brand that says what the score says. Match Moth is a moth steering by a truer light through a noisy night, discernment rather than attraction to the brightest thing, and the promise underneath is four words: match better, apply less.

The system that came out of it is small and strict. Deep navy is the canvas, never pure black, because aqua, cream and gold only look saturated against it. Teal means action or positive evidence. Gold is reserved for Standout and warm brand light, and a match is never graded red, yellow, green. Spectral speaks for anything that is a job, a listing or a score. The app's own chrome speaks in Plus Jakarta. Every color and type rule lives in one token file and renders on the design route, so a component that drifts is visible before it ships. The moth mark shipped on September 7.

Accessibility got the same treatment as the score: measured, then reported honestly. An August audit ran keyboard and contrast checks against production at desktop and phone sizes. It passed on focus, with no traps and a visible focus state on every control. It failed on six components under the contrast floor, the worst being the score badge itself at 2.46:1, and on form errors that were announced to nobody on seven of nine surfaces. Names, landmarks and error announcements were fixed the same day. Contrast now gates the build, after 107 violations across 34 files were cleared. A match is never graded by color alone, and a screen-reader pass is still to do.

Process

Spec-driven development with AI

AI writes most of the code, so the documentation is the project's memory. Every phase starts as a spec: the problem, the measurements behind it, the plan, and a decisions log where each ruling gets a dated row so no later session re-argues it. 78 specs have shipped and 27 are waiting, which is the backlog. Every ship adds a changelog entry with the reason and the before-and-after numbers, over 1,000 entries in five months, alongside 58 research documents and 144 QA reports. Agents build overnight on a branch against the spec, a Playwright run checks the golden path, and every morning starts with my eyes on what landed. AI doesn't flag its own bad decisions. You find them by paying attention.

Outcome

One month of open beta

As of September 21: 53 real signups, 20 onboarded, and 43 apply clicks.

Retention isn't measurable yet. The first four-week cohort lands in late September, and this page gets a revision in mid-October with that data, an impression log, and skip reasons.

What the product can't fix: employer silence and the market's odds. No seeker-side tool makes a recruiter reply.

The question this first month answers is the one the page started with. Can I design and ship an AI product that strangers trust enough to act on? Real users have clicked through to apply 43 times. The evidence is above.
